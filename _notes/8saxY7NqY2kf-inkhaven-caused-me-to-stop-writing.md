Before Inkhaven, I only published when I was inspired to. I would do or discover something, decide it was worth writing about, start writing, and 3-4 hours later have published a blog post. I didn't have a goal of publishing before, it was just a bonus when it happened. More than anything else, I applied to Inkhaven because I was writing quite a bit and thought it would be good to publish more of my writing.

At Inkhaven, inspiration was rare. Without inspiration, writing was qualitatively different. There were so many things I wanted to do, interesting people to talk to, and engineering projects I wanted to engage in. I often denied myself them — I was at Inkhaven to write/publish and I should do that first and then enjoy the rest of the day. But my mind was smarter than me; forbidden from doing what I actually wanted, it plagued me with less healthy distraction and procrastination, and I often ended up publishing just before the deadline.

Nevertheless, the first few weeks of publishing were exhilarating. I managed to publish every day, and I even managed to write several pieces I was proud of[^proud-of]. With about 10 days left in the residency, I'd gained confidence in my ability as a writer and a sense of belonging at Inkhaven. Inkhaven taught me that sitting down with the intention to write/publish was sufficient to publish something with just a few hours of effort.

[^proud-of]: Usually pieces I'd felt inspired to write. [AirPods](/VPDDYgykGJvl/airpods), [Review: The 6% Club](/GrMhcXX3CY7s/review-the-6-club), [空色♡ Birthday Card](/mZ5sitead5kB/空色-birthday-card), and [5 unexpectedly mindful activities](/shWm4speAqvp/5-unexpectedly-mindful-activities) all come to mind.

However as Inkhaven progressed, writing was interesting me less and less. My most valuable experiences were interactions with other residents or the non-writing activities like hiking Muir Woods. These experiences rarely inspired me to write — I often didn't think I had 500 words to write about them, they felt too personal, or didn't feel novel enough.

My motivation and inspiration to write disappeared over the last days of Inkhaven. I would often procrastinate writing late into the day, I felt FOMO towards the nighttime activities I was missing, and the quality of my writing declined as I was spending less time on it.

The last ~10 days of Inkhaven I'd ceased to write because I wanted to. Writing no longer was a terminal goal of mine, it was merely instrumental to me being able to stay at Inkhaven for the remainder of the residency.

For the first several months after Inkhaven, I lived under the delusion that I wanted to ramp back up and at least publish more often than I had before Inkhaven. However, rather than focus on publishing, I spent a lot of time journaling/personal notes. The first deadline for Foreverhaven came and went and I couldn't even bring myself to edit/finish my [review of Inkhaven](/PK2boIoMk82X/inkhaven-is-two-events-stabled-into-one) that I'd published unfinished the last day before the closing ceremony. I felt trapped between wanting to write higher quality pieces than I had in the last days of Inkhaven (no daily publishing requirement meant that I could theoretically devote more time to each blog post) and wanting to reduce the friction I needed to publish.

As the months wore on, it became harder to lie to myself: I didn't care about publishing lengthier higher quality think pieces or resuming my Foreverhaven streak.

---

> Thinking about trying to do an Inkhaven like writing month again this month. I should have plenty of time in theory.
> - me, in my notes/journal, August 1st 2026

I did less writing in the subsequent month than the past 5 years. The contrast between telling myself I wanted to publish blog posts and my lack of desire to write freed me of my promise to write/publish more often.

I subsequently stopped not only writing publicly but also all my journaling and lifelogging. I think this was profoundly good for me. The requirement to write down everything I did in a day I was upholding myself to had been leading to a kind of reward hack: if I do nothing or just watch TV all day, I wouldn't have to write as much down.

A large part of my motivation to publish was that I was writing a lot, and it seemed a shame not to publish it. Gwern emphasized this, challenging me to make publishing my default rather than a separate opt-in step. In a way, I already believed this, my only private Github repos are my journal's source plus repos containing intellectual property that I do not own.

But my mistake, was not publishing more often, it was conflating my notes/journal with writing that I intended to publish. I was tricking myself into thinking that my writing would aggregate into something publishable, instead of focusing on creating something that I wanted to publish in the first place.

Part of me still wants to publish things more often. However, despite having written ~1000 words a day at some point in my life, I'd no longer consider myself a
primarily writer. Instead, I'm reevaluating the things I regularly create to see whether they are publishable[^photos], plus considering publishiability as a criteria for what seems worth creating / spending time on.

[^photos]: For example, I recently started publishing [photos I take / caption](https://unformeddelta.wiki/tagged/photo).
